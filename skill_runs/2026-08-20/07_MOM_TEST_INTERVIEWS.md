# Mom Test Interview Guide — Current Business

Applied existing skill: `skills/vendor/getagentseal_founder-playbook/mom-test/SKILL.md`

## Purpose

The goal is not to ask whether people like the current idea. The goal is to learn how parents and children actually behave today around household help, initiative, allowance, refusal, and problem noticing.

Do not pitch the product during the discovery portion.

## Three biggest learning goals

1. Does the underlying problem exist strongly enough in real households?
2. What currently happens when a child is asked to help, refuses, negotiates, or acts without being asked?
3. Is there evidence that parents already invest time/money/effort trying to cultivate initiative, making them plausible early customers?

---

# Parent interview

## Opening frame

Keep the conversation casual. Explain that you are trying to understand how families handle everyday household situations and children’s initiative; you are not asking them to judge an app idea.

## Core questions

### Recent real behavior

- “Tell me about the last time you wanted your child to help with something at home.”
- “What exactly did you say?”
- “What did your child do next?”
- “What happened if they did not want to do it?”
- “How did the situation end?”

### Initiative

- “Tell me about the most recent time your child noticed something and helped without being asked.”
- “What do you think made them notice it that time?”
- “How did you respond?”
- “Did anything similar happen again later?”

### Negotiation / refusal

- “When was the last time your child tried to change the way, timing, or conditions of something you asked them to do?”
- “What did they ask for?”
- “How did you decide whether to accept it?”

### Current solutions/workarounds

- “How do you currently decide who does what at home?”
- “Do you use any charts, apps, calendars, rules, or routines?”
- “What have you tried in the past that did not last?”
- “What was annoying or difficult about those methods?”

### Allowance / compensation

- “How do you currently give allowance?”
- “Tell me about the last time money was connected to a household task.”
- “Who decided the amount?”
- “Has your child ever asked for a different amount or condition?”
- “What happened?”

### Importance / existing investment

- “Have you ever spent money, bought a book/tool, joined a program, or changed a family routine because you wanted your child to become more independent or proactive?”
- “What did you choose?”
- “How much effort or money did it take?”
- “Did it work?”

### Consequence

- “What bothers you most about how this works today?”
- “What happens if nothing changes?”

### Commitment / next step

Only after discovery:
- Ask whether they would be willing to participate in one real household test.
- If they participate and see value, later offer a concrete second session or paid pilot.
- Ask for an introduction to another parent only if the conversation showed real relevance.

Do not count compliments as commitment.

# Child conversation / observation

For children, avoid turning the interview into a formal questionnaire. Prefer a short conversation around recent events and, where appropriate, direct observation of a real situation.

## Recent self-initiated action

- “What is the last thing you did at home without someone telling you first?”
- “How did you notice it needed doing?”
- “Why did you decide to do it?”
- “What happened after you did it?”

## Recent instruction

- “What is the last thing someone at home told you to do that you did not really want to do?”
- “What did they say?”
- “What did you do?”
- “Were you allowed to do it a different way or later?”

## Help / uncertainty

- “When you see someone busy at home, how do you decide whether there is something you can do?”
- “What do you do when you are not sure how to help?”

## Reward

- “Tell me about the last time you got money or a reward for doing something at home.”
- “Did you know the reward before you started?”
- “Would you have done it without the reward?”
- Follow the answer with “Why?” and concrete past examples rather than arguing about motivation.

## Feedback

- “What is the last thing someone said after you helped that made you feel good or made you understand what you did well?”
- “What did they say exactly?”

## Observation rule

Behavior matters more than the child saying “this sounds fun.” If a prototype is shown later, measure whether the child continues, proposes, changes, refuses, or asks to repeat.

# Grandparent interview — later priority

Grandparents are not required to be the first requester in the current design, so initial discovery should prioritize parent-child behavior. When grandparent involvement is tested, ask about actual past behavior:

- “When was the last time you talked with your grandchild about something they had done recently?”
- “How did you hear about it?”
- “What did you say back?”
- “When was the last time you asked your grandchild to do or find something for you?”
- “What happened?”
- “Do you currently send allowance/gifts? Tell me about the last time.”

Avoid “Would you use an app to send quests?”

---

# Questions to avoid

Do not ask:
- “Do you think this is a good idea?”
- “Would your child use this?”
- “Would you pay for this?” as the primary evidence
- “Do you want your child to be more independent?”
- “Would grandparents like this?”
- “How much would you pay?” before any concrete offer

These invite politeness and hypothetical answers.

# Re-anchoring phrases

When someone says:
- “Usually…” → “What happened the last time?”
- “I would definitely…” → “Have you done anything like that before?”
- “That sounds great.” → “How do you handle this today?”
- “You should add X.” → “What would that help you do? Tell me about the last time you needed that.”

# Evidence capture after each interview

Record separately:

1. Recent concrete behaviors
2. Current workaround/alternative
3. Money/time already spent
4. Emotional pain or friction observed
5. Exact contradictions to the business hypothesis
6. Commitments offered (time, referral, money)
7. What belief changed because of the interview

# Stop / segment rule

If interviews produce wildly different behavior, do not average them. Slice the segment more narrowly and identify the parents/children for whom the problem is most concrete and frequent.

Stop relying on interviews alone once multiple households show the same pattern and real pilot behavior/commitments are available. At that point, move from problem discovery to experiments.
