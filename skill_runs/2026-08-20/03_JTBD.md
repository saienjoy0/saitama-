# Jobs To Be Done — Provisional Decision Artifact

Applied existing skill: `skills/vendor/tjboudreaux_cc-thinking-skills/thinking-jobs-to-be-done/SKILL.md`

## Evidence status

The current business specification is rich in product intent but does **not** yet contain enough direct customer interview evidence to claim validated Jobs To Be Done. Therefore all job statements below are **provisional hypotheses**, not customer-validated findings. Do not invent quotes.

## Primary job hypothesis — child

**Job statement**

> When something in my family’s daily life could be improved but I am not sure what I should do, I want enough help to notice what is happening and enough freedom to decide what I can do, so I can feel that I found and delivered something useful myself rather than merely obeying an instruction.

### Performer
- Primary: child
- Frequency: potentially recurring in daily family situations, but not yet measured
- Stakes: emotional/autonomy/learning stakes appear central in the product thesis; actual importance to children remains unvalidated

### Functional dimension
- Understand a real situation
- Decide what can be done
- Negotiate method/conditions/help
- Carry out an action
- See whether it actually helped

### Emotional dimension — hypothesis
- Avoid feeling controlled or merely ordered around
- Gain a sense of “I noticed / I figured it out / I helped”
- Feel capable of making decisions

### Social dimension — hypothesis
- Be recognized by family for concrete value delivered rather than generic praise

## Secondary job hypothesis — parent

**Job statement**

> When I want my child to become more proactive in everyday life but I do not want to constantly instruct, nag, or solve the problem for them, I want a lightweight way to give an entry point and safe boundaries while leaving meaningful decisions to the child, so I can support growth without turning every household situation into another command.

### Performer
- Parent is currently the leading requester and payer hypothesis.
- Actual willingness to invest time or money is unvalidated.

## Later job hypothesis — grandparent

**Job statement**

> When I want to be meaningfully involved in my grandchild’s everyday growth, I want a concrete way to respond, ask, share experience, or create the next challenge, so I can contribute to what happens next rather than only receiving finished photos.

This should remain secondary during initial validation because the current business specification explicitly allows the first loop to start with parents.

## Forces — provisional

### Push from current state
Hypotheses to test:
- Repeated direct instructions may produce resistance or passive compliance.
- Parents may struggle to create room for initiative while still keeping household tasks practical and safe.
- Children may lack a clear starting point for noticing useful opportunities in ordinary life.

### Pull toward proposed progress
- Child chooses and modifies instead of only receiving commands.
- Child can see a real person-specific result from their action.
- Family feedback can turn an action into learning for the next attempt.

### Anxiety blocking switching
- Parent worries the process takes longer than simply giving instructions.
- Child may perceive reflection as homework.
- Parent may not know how much freedom is appropriate.
- Payment/negotiation may create friction.
- AI interaction may be boring, confusing, or overly directive.

### Habit/status quo
Potential existing workarounds to investigate:
- verbal instructions
- chore charts/checklists
- fixed or task-based allowance
- parent doing the task themselves
- praise after spontaneous help
- family messaging/photo sharing
- existing chore/allowance apps

Specific products and actual usage frequency must be verified through interviews rather than inferred.

## Competition

### Direct category competition
- chore/task management products
- allowance/reward management products

### Indirect competition
- paper charts
- shared family calendars/checklists
- messaging apps
- parent reminders
- verbal negotiation

### Non-consumption
- parent simply does the task
- child has no role in the situation
- family does not intentionally create a learning loop

The most important competition may be **the speed of simply telling the child what to do**. This is a critical hypothesis: the new loop must create enough perceived developmental or relational value to justify extra steps.

## Outcomes

### Minimize
- number of adult answer-giving interventions required
- time before the child can make a meaningful decision
- parent setup burden
- coercive “do this for money” dynamics
- unnecessary AI dependence

### Maximize
- child-initiated observations
- child-proposed actions
- modifications/negotiations initiated by the child
- family feedback that specifies concrete value created
- voluntary repeat attempts
- share of attempts completed with less scaffolding than before

## Priority implication

### Build / test now
Focus on the smallest interaction that serves the child’s hypothesized job:

**real situation → notice → interpret → propose → negotiate → act → receive concrete value feedback → reflect**

Do not prioritize features that do not materially improve this job until the job itself is validated.

### Cut/defer from core validation
- full family newspaper generation
- complex points economy
- in-app payments
- rankings/social network
- broad regional integrations
- grandparent-first onboarding

## Strongest countercase

The child may not “hire” any solution for autonomy or problem discovery at all. The actual child job may simply be:

> “Make chores faster, easier, or more rewarding.”

If observed behavior shows that children only engage when the activity is immediately game-like or materially rewarded, while the deeper observation/proposal loop reduces engagement, the current job hypothesis is wrong or applies only to a narrower segment.

A second countercase is that the **parent**, not the child, is the actual primary job performer because the service is bought to change parenting behavior rather than to solve a felt child problem.

Both countercases must be tested.

## Evidence gaps

Highest-priority research gaps:
1. Last real examples of a child voluntarily noticing and helping.
2. Last real examples of parental instruction/resistance.
3. What the child says made them act without being told.
4. Whether parents already try methods that preserve choice/autonomy.
5. Whether parents consider this problem important enough to spend time and money on.
6. What children themselves consider “fun,” “useful,” or “annoying” in the proposed flow.
